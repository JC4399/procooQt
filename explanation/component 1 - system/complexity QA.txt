Q: what is complexity? what is the difference between something that is complicated and complex?
A:
Complexity is a property of a system that arises when many parts interact in ways that are interdependent, nonlinear, and feedback-driven, such that the system's behaviour is emergent — it cannot be derived by examining the parts in isolation. A complex system is more than the sum of its parts not as a poetic flourish but literally: the interesting behaviour lives in the relationships, not the components.

You can also treat complexity as a quantity — how much irreducible structure/description a system requires (Kolmogorov complexity, entropy, etc.) — but in a systems/engineering context the working definition is: the degree to which a system's behaviour is unpredictable and irreducible from knowledge of its parts.

Complicated vs. complex

The distinction is not "how many parts" — both can have many. It's about the nature of the interactions and therefore what you can know and do.

- Interactions: Complicated = linear, deterministic, fixed | Complex = nonlinear, feedback loops, adaptive
- Predictability: Complicated = predictable given enough analysis | Complex = fundamentally unpredictable; only patterns/tendencies
- Reducibility: Complicated = can be taken apart and reassembled | Complex = disassembly destroys the behaviour; whole ≠ sum of parts
- Cause & effect: Complicated = knowable in advance | Complex = only knowable in hindsight
- Repeatability: Complicated = same input → same output | Complex = same input → often different output
- Adaptation: Complicated = doesn't learn or self-modify | Complex = adapts, learns, co-evolves with environment
- How you deal with it: Complicated = engineer it; apply expertise/best practice | Complex = probe it; safe-to-fail experiments; nudge, don't "solve"

The clean test: a complicated system, given enough expertise, is fully knowable — you can model it, predict it, and fix it by analysis. An aircraft engine, a tax code, a Swiss watch: many parts, but deterministic and decomposable. A complex system is one where no amount of component-level analysis lets you predict the whole — a market, an ecosystem, an immune system, a team, a supply chain under stress. Complexity is irreducible; complicatedness is merely unanalysed.

Put bluntly: complicated means "hard but knowable"; complex means "knowable only in part, and only after the fact."

The cake, the rocket, the child — and the 17 goals: four rungs of increasing order

These are not three (or four) problems of increasing size; they are problems of increasing kind — each rung is a higher order of complexity than the one below it.

- Cake — simple. You follow a recipe: steps in order, fixed ingredients, predictable result. Anyone with the recipe gets a cake. Repeat it and you get the same cake. The "how" is written down and it works. This is the realm of best practice — just follow the steps.

- Rocket — complicated. Rocket science. Many interdependent subsystems, deep expertise required, and getting it wrong is catastrophic. But it is deterministic physics: if you get every parameter right, it works, and you can analyse, model, test and verify it before launch. It's decomposable — teams build the engine, the guidance, the fuel separately and bolt them together. The whole is knowable from the parts. This is the realm of experts and good analysis. Hard, but knowable.

- Child — complex. Raising a child. There is no recipe that works twice — not because we lack the recipe, but because no fixed recipe can exist. The child adapts, responds to you as you respond to them, each child is different, and the outcome is only understood in hindsight. This is the realm of probe, adapt, nudge. But notice: a child is still one complex system, with a coherent set of actors (a family), a shared-enough goal (a thriving adult), a responsible agent (the parent), and bounded stakes. It is complex, but it is contained.

- The 17 goals — a higher order still: a system of coupled complex systems (a "wicked" problem). The UN's 17 Sustainable Development Goals (poverty, hunger, health, education, gender, water, energy, growth, infrastructure, inequality, cities, consumption, climate, oceans, land, justice, partnerships) are not one complex system like a child. They are seventeen complex systems that are all coupled to each other, and that coupling is exactly why they are far harder than any child. Concretely, why they sit above the child rung:

  1. Not one system, but a system of systems. A child is a single adaptive system. The goals are interacting economies, ecosystems, health systems, food systems, energy systems, governance systems — each complex in its own right, and each embedded in and feeding back into the others. Complexity compounds when you couple complex systems together.

  2. The goals trade off against each other. You cannot optimise one without perturbing the rest. Cheap energy (Goal 7) pushes against climate (Goal 13). More food (Goal 2) pushes against biodiversity and land (Goals 14–15). Growth (Goal 8) pushes against inequality (Goal 10) and consumption (Goal 12). Progress here is regression there — there is no single lever, only an interaction web where every pull has cross-effects that are themselves hard to predict.

  3. No shared objective — the goal is contested. Raising a child has a broadly shared aim. "Sustainable development" does not: 193 governments, corporations, communities and 8 billion people hold different, often conflicting definitions of success, values, and priorities. The "problem" is not given; it is argued over. That is the mark of a wicked problem, not merely a complex one.

  4. No controller. A child has a parent. The goals have no one in charge — governance is fragmented, distributed, and sometimes adversarial. There is no single decision-maker who can pull a lever and steer the whole.

  5. Delayed, diffuse, and irreversible feedback. Effects unfold over decades; you cannot run a controlled experiment or reset and retry; and some thresholds are irreversible (climate tipping points, species loss). The cost of error is civilizational, not a scraped knee.

  6. Non-stationarity — the system changes while you act on it. A child grows; the SDG landscape morphs — climate shifts, technology shifts, norms and geopolitics shift — so the target keeps moving under the intervention.

  7. No stopping rule. There is no clear test of when a goal is "solved," and every attempt to fix one part redefines the problem elsewhere. This is precisely Rittel & Webber's definition of a wicked problem: no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, no true/false test of a solution — only better or worse, judged after the fact.

  So the 17 goals are not a child; they are many children, all holding hands, each pulling the others in a different direction, with no parent in the room and the ground moving underneath. If you force them onto the ladder, they push past "complex" toward the wicked / super-complex end — the point where the complexity is no longer just in the system but in the coupling between systems, the contest over goals, and the absence of any controller.

The full ladder:

- Realm: Cake = Simple | Rocket = Complicated | Child = Complex | 17 Goals = Wicked / system-of-systems
- Number of systems: Cake = one, trivial | Rocket = one | Child = one complex system | 17 Goals = many coupled complex systems
- Recipe?: Cake = follow it | Rocket = engineer it | Child = none exists | 17 Goals = none — and the goal itself is contested
- Repeatable?: Cake = same cake every time | Rocket = yes, if parameters right | Child = never the same twice | 17 Goals = no — and each attempt changes the problem
- Knowable in advance?: Cake = yes | Rocket = yes, by analysis | Child = no — only in hindsight | 17 Goals = no — distributed, delayed, some effects irreversible
- Who is in control?: Cake = you | Rocket = you (the engineers) | Child = a parent | 17 Goals = no one
- How you deal with it: Cake = best practice | Rocket = expertise / analysis | Child = sense, experiment, adapt | 17 Goals = wicked-problem handling: many actors, safe-to-fail probes, adaptive governance, accept you can only steer, never solve

In sum: complexity is emergent, irreducible behaviour from interdependent parts. Simple is a recipe (the cake); complicated is hard but knowable (the rocket); complex is knowable only in part and only after the fact, yet still one contained system with a responsible agent (the child). The 17 goals are a higher order again — not a complex system but a coupled network of complex systems with contested goals, no controller, delayed and partly irreversible feedback, and no stopping rule — a wicked problem. The failure mode at every rung is treating it as the rung below: a rocket as a cake, a child as a rocket, and the 17 goals as a child you can simply "raise" with a plan.

Q: what is Boulding hierarchy of complexity by Kenneth Boulding? explain.
A:
Kenneth Boulding set it out in his 1956 paper "General Systems Theory: The Skeleton of Science." It's one of the founding documents of General Systems Theory. The idea: systems in the world are not all of one kind — they come in levels of increasing complexity, and each level introduces a new organising principle that does not exist at the levels below it. He proposed nine levels, from the simplest static structure up to the unknowable.

The nine levels:

1. Frameworks / static structures — Pattern, arrangement in space; nothing moving. Example: a crystal, the atom's structure, a map, anatomy. Studied by geography, anatomy, crystallography.

2. Clockworks / simple dynamic systems — Predetermined, necessary motion; predictable dynamics. Example: the solar system, a clock, a machine. Studied by classical physics, mechanics.

3. Control mechanisms / cybernetic systems — Self-regulating via feedback; maintains equilibrium. Example: a thermostat, a governor, homeostasis. Studied by cybernetics, control theory.

4. Open systems / self-maintaining structures — Maintains itself against decay by exchanging matter/energy with environment; metabolism, boundary, self-reproduction. Example: the flame, the cell. Studied by biology.

5. Genetic-societal (plant) — Division of labour among cells; growth, differentiation, reproduction from a "blueprint." Example: the plant. Studied by botany.

6. Animal — Mobility, goal-directed (teleological) behaviour, specialised sense organs, a nervous system, learning. Example: the animal. Studied by zoology.

7. Human — Self-consciousness, symbolic language, self-reflection, produces/absorbs/interprets complex symbolic information; values, art. Example: the individual person. Studied by psychology.

8. Social organisation — Human organisations with roles, communication, coordination, shared values, culture. Example: a firm, a society, an economy. Studied by sociology, economics.

9. Transcendental systems — The "inescapable," ultimate, absolute — the unknowable; systems of unspecified structure. Example: the transcendent. Studied by theology, philosophy.

What the hierarchy is actually saying

1. Emergence, not just accumulation. Each level is not "more of the same" — it brings a genuinely new property the level below lacks. A clockwork (2) can't explain self-maintenance (4); a cell (4) can't explain goal-directed learning (6); an animal (6) can't explain self-consciousness (7). This is the anti-reductionist core: you cannot explain a higher level purely in the vocabulary of a lower one. It's the same intuition behind the cake/rocket/child ladder — the whole is a different kind of thing, not just a bigger thing.

2. Knowledge thins as you climb. Boulding's sharp observation: we understand the bottom of the ladder (physics, mechanics) with great precision and the top (society, the transcendent) only vaguely. The hierarchy is also a map of our ignorance — the higher the level, the less rigorous our science.

3. A "skeleton of science." Its purpose was organisational: to give each discipline a home on the ladder, reveal the gaps between levels, and suggest where a general systems theory could find laws that hold across levels. It was a research agenda, not a finished taxonomy.

4. Each level is a research frontier. Boulding noted that a level is "understood" only when you can build a theory of it — and that some levels (3, the cybernetic) were the newest and most promising at the time.

Q: what is the history of people's attempts to solve complex systems? I know about operations research, the viable system model, soft systems methodology, and actor-network theory. What milestones am I missing, and what and why did these milestones fail and lead to the next stage?
A:
There is a single pattern running through this entire history. Someone invents a method for making a tangled problem tractable; the method works well on the kind of problem it was built for; then it meets a different kind of problem that it structurally cannot handle; and the failure of that method is what pushes the field to invent the next one. The direction of travel is always the same. It begins with the belief that the system is "out there" as an objective thing you can measure and optimise, then shifts to the belief that the system actually lives in people's heads as competing interpretations you must negotiate, then shifts again to the belief that the system is emergent and can only be probed and nudged, and finally arrives at the present view that you must match your approach to the situation rather than swear allegiance to one method. In what follows, the approaches you already know are marked as familiar, and the ones you were missing are flagged.

Cybernetics — Norbert Wiener, 1948 (you were missing this; it is the root of everything after)

Cybernetics began with a very concrete wartime problem: how do you aim a gun at an aircraft that is moving and that you cannot see clearly? Wiener, working on fire-control systems, realised that hitting a moving target is really a problem of prediction and correction — you estimate where the target will be, fire, watch where the shot went, and correct your aim, over and over. He then saw that this same loop of measuring, comparing, and correcting is how living things stay alive (the body holds its temperature steady the same way) and how machines regulate themselves. He generalised the insight into a science of "control and communication in the animal and the machine," and Ashby later added the key law of the field. The purpose of cybernetics was to find the common principles by which any system — a machine, an organism, an organisation — steers itself toward a goal in the face of disturbance. The method is the feedback loop: you measure the current state, compare it with the desired state, and act on the difference, and you keep doing this continuously. Ashby's law of requisite variety says, in plain terms, that a controller can only keep a system under control if it has at least as many possible responses as the system has possible disturbances; if the disturbances are more varied than the controller's repertoire, the controller is overwhelmed and loses control. Cybernetics fell short as a way of solving complex problems because it assumes the goal is already known and given. It tells you how to steer toward a goal, but it has no account of where the goal comes from, what it means, or whose goal it is. That single blind spot is why the field split into two branches: those who took the goal as fixed and set about optimising it, which became operations research, and those who insisted the goal itself is contested and must be negotiated, which became the soft methods.

General Systems Theory — Bertalanffy and Boulding, 1950s (you were missing this)

After the Second World War the sciences had fragmented into narrow specialities, each with its own vocabulary and its own journals, and people worried that no one could see the whole. Bertalanffy, a biologist, noticed that systems in wildly different fields share the same underlying patterns: an organism, a company, and a flame all maintain themselves by exchanging material with their environment; many different starting points can lead to the same end state; and all of them grow, reach limits, and decay. Boulding tried to arrange these shared patterns into a ladder of increasingly complex systems, from crystals up to societies. The purpose of General Systems Theory was to create a common language and a set of general laws that would hold for systems of every kind, so that a biologist, an engineer, and a sociologist could actually talk to one another. Its method, however, was entirely conceptual: it identified shared ideas such as open versus closed systems, homeostasis, and equifinality (the fact that a system can reach the same goal from different starting points), but it offered no procedure you could actually carry out on a real problem. That is exactly why it fell short. It remained a philosophy rather than a technique. It produced no testable predictions and no method of application, and "system" became such a broad word that saying something was a system explained nothing in particular. Its failure forced the field to stop dreaming of a universal theory and start building concrete methods that could actually be used — which is where operations research and system dynamics came in.

Operations Research and Systems Analysis — 1940s to 1960s (you knew this)

Operations research was born in wartime Britain, where scientists such as Blackett were drafted to help the military make better decisions with scarce resources: how large should a convoy be, how deep should depth charges explode, how should radar be deployed. After the war the same approach moved into the RAND Corporation as "systems analysis" and then into the American Pentagon under McNamara, and from there into industry and government. Its purpose was to replace intuition and guesswork with mathematics, so that a decision-maker could choose the provably best option among the alternatives available. The method is straightforward to describe: you build a mathematical model of the situation, you define a single objective to maximise or minimise (say, minimise cost or maximise damage to the enemy), you list the constraints you must stay within, and then you solve the resulting equations — using techniques such as linear programming — to find the optimum. Operations research fell short because the whole approach rests on the assumption that the problem is well-defined and that everyone agrees on a single, measurable objective. It is genuinely excellent on "tame" problems such as scheduling, logistics, and inventory, where those assumptions hold. But it collapses on messy, political, multi-stakeholder problems, for two reasons. First, it can only optimise what it can measure, so it ends up optimising the measurable and ignoring what actually matters — the classic example being McNamara's Vietnam "body counts," which measured how many enemy were killed rather than whether the war was being won, and the systems-analysis of social programs that optimised the wrong quantities. Second, even when it produces a genuinely optimal answer, that answer is often simply ignored because it is politically unacceptable or because no one was consulted in producing it — the so-called implementation gap. The wall it hit is the realisation that you cannot optimise a problem you have not correctly framed, and that framing a problem is not a technical act at all. That wall split the field in two: some wanted to model the feedback and delay that OR ignored, which led to system dynamics, and others wanted to deal with the human mess, which led to the soft methods.

System Dynamics — Forrester and Meadows, 1950s to 1970s (you were missing this)

System dynamics was the creation of Jay Forrester, an engineer at MIT who had built real feedback-control hardware and then turned the same thinking onto companies, then onto cities, and finally onto the entire world; his group's work fed into the famous Club of Rome report The Limits to Growth in 1972. Its purpose was to explain why complex systems so often behave counterintuitively, why a policy that seems obviously helpful can make things worse in the long run, and to let people explore the long-term consequences of their choices before making them in reality. The method is built from three ideas. A "stock" is something that accumulates, like the amount of pollution in the air or the number of people in a city; a "flow" is the rate at which it fills or drains, like emissions per year or births per year; and "feedback loops with delays" are the ways that a change in a stock eventually loops back to change the flows, but only after some delay. You write these relationships down as equations and let a computer simulate them over many years, then you test different policies inside the simulation and watch what happens. System dynamics fell short because, for all its sophistication, it is still a single quantitative model built by a single group of experts, whose boundaries and equations and numbers are all assumptions they chose. It cannot hold several conflicting worldviews at once, since there is only one model. Its predictions were and remain fiercely contested, as the attacks on The Limits to Growth showed, because reasonable people disagreed about the assumptions baked into it. And it models people as mechanical variables inside feedback loops, with no real agency, no interpretation, and no politics, so it can tell you how the model behaves but not how to intervene among people who disagree about what the model should even contain.

The Viable System Model and organisational cybernetics — Stafford Beer, 1970s (you knew this)

Stafford Beer took cybernetics and applied it to the management of organisations, and he pushed it to its most ambitious extreme in Project Cybersyn in Chile between 1971 and 1973, where he tried to build a real-time computer system to manage an entire national economy for the Allende government; the project died when the government was overthrown by the 1973 coup. His purpose was to be able to diagnose whether an organisation is capable of surviving in a turbulent world, and to design it so that it can. The method rests on the claim that any system that survives must perform five distinct functions. It must do its actual work (the operations); it must coordinate its parts so they do not fight each other; it must manage and optimise its internal operations day to day; it must look outward and think about the future and adapt to a changing environment; and it must hold a coherent sense of its own identity and purpose. Crucially, Beer insisted these five functions recur at every level — every unit inside the organisation is itself a whole viable system with its own five functions — and that each level must command enough variety to cope with the variety it faces, which is Ashby's law applied to management. The model fell short because it is structural and prescriptive: it tells you which functions must exist, but it does not tell you how to negotiate meaning or how to handle power. It assumes the organisation has a coherent purpose, whereas in reality purpose is usually contested; it is technocratic and top-down; and it ignores the fact that different stakeholders see the organisation differently. It is a diagnostic and design framework, not a participative process, and it simply cannot cope with the fact that people disagree about what the problem actually is.

Soft Systems Methodology and soft OR — Checkland, and Eden's SODA, 1970s and 1980s (you knew this)

Soft systems methodology was developed by Peter Checkland at Lancaster, who had worked in hard systems engineering and watched it fail again and again on messy management problems, and it represents the "soft" turn in British systems thinking; Eden and Ackermann's Strategic Options Development and Analysis was a parallel soft-OR approach. Its purpose was to help groups of people who disagree about a messy situation to make progress anyway, by surfacing their different assumptions and finding enough common ground to act, rather than pretending there is one objective problem to be solved. The method is a structured process. You begin by drawing a "rich picture," an informal cartoon capturing the mess, the players, and the conflicts. Then, for each important viewpoint, you write a "root definition," a careful statement of a relevant system as seen from that viewpoint, which is checked against the CATWOE checklist (who benefits, who does the work, what transformation is involved, what worldview is assumed, who owns it, and what environment constrains it). From each root definition you build a conceptual model of what that ideal system would do, you compare that model against messy reality, and you use the differences to drive a structured debate aimed at "accommodation" — an agreement people can live with, not a forced consensus. The whole point is that "systems" are mental constructs rather than objective facts, and different people legitimately construct them differently. It fell short because it brackets power. It assumes the stakeholders are identifiable, willing to take part, and roughly equal, so the accommodation it reaches can quietly paper over the fact that some parties dominate others — the substance of the critique levelled by Habermas and by Jackson. It is also slow and process-heavy, it tends to produce learning and talk rather than decisions and action, it does not predict how the system will actually behave, and it offers no guarantee that anything will change. The wall it hit — that it handles multiple perspectives but ignores structure and power — is what produced the critical turn.

Critical Systems Thinking — Jackson and Flood, 1980s and 1990s (you were missing this)

Critical systems thinking was the British systems community's response to the discovery that soft systems methodology had quietly ignored power, and it drew heavily on Habermas's critical social theory; its best-known products are Jackson and Flood's Total Systems Intervention and their "System of Systems Methodologies" grid. Its purpose was twofold: to be methodologically pluralistic, by consciously choosing the right method for the situation instead of defending one favourite, and to be emancipatory, by exposing and confronting power and coercion rather than merely accommodating whoever is in the room. The method works by classifying the situation first. The grid sorts contexts along two axes — whether they are simple or complex, and whether the people involved are unitary (sharing goals), pluralist (differing but reconcilable), or coercive (in genuine conflict, with some oppressing others) — and then it points you toward the kind of method that fits, so that hard methods suit unitary contexts, soft methods suit pluralist ones, and critical methods are needed for coercive ones. Total Systems Intervention adds three phases of creativity, choice, and implementation. It fell short because it is a meta-methodology, a framework for choosing frameworks, and therefore heavy and abstract to actually apply. Its critique of power is more diagnosis than operation, it does not itself generate solutions to real problems, and its categories are coarse. It runs alongside complexity science, but the practical, everyday synthesis of "match your stance to the situation" only really arrived later, with Cynefin.

Complexity science and the Santa Fe school — 1980s and 1990s (you were missing this)

Complexity science took shape at the Santa Fe Institute, founded in 1984, which deliberately gathered physicists, biologists, economists, and computer scientists in one place because they had noticed something strange: a whole family of systems — ant colonies, economies, immune systems, ecosystems, brains, stock markets — look nothing alike yet behave in strikingly similar ways. All of them consist of many parts interacting locally, none of them has a central controller telling the parts what to do, and yet all of them produce ordered, adaptive, and often surprising behaviour. Traditional science, which works by breaking a system into its parts and solving equations for the whole, could not cope with systems like this, so the field set out to build new tools. Its purpose was to explain how order, adaptation, and novelty can arise from local interaction alone, without anyone being in charge — a phenomenon called emergence. Its method is best understood through its main tools. Agent-based modelling abandons the idea of writing one equation for the whole system and instead writes simple rules for many individual "agents," lets them interact on a computer, and then watches what pattern appears at the level of the whole; the crucial point is that the aggregate pattern is never programmed in, it emerges from the interactions. The study of fitness landscapes pictures adaptation as a climb up a landscape whose height represents how well something performs, and shows that when the landscape is rugged, with many separate peaks, an evolving system gets trapped on a mediocre local peak and cannot reach the true best solution, which explains why optimisation is so hard in these systems. The theory of self-organised criticality, developed by Bak, showed that some systems drive themselves into a precarious state, like a sandpile that builds up until a single added grain triggers an avalanche, and that in such a state events of every size can happen, which explains why these systems produce occasional catastrophic collapses. Cellular automata and artificial life showed that a handful of simple local rules, applied over and over on a grid, can generate enormous complexity, which demonstrated concretely how complexity can come from simplicity. Woven through all of this were the central themes of emergence, adaptation, nonlinearity, and the idea that systems poised on the boundary between rigid order and total chaos are the most adaptive. Complexity science fell short because much of it stayed in the realm of toy models — abstract agents in a computer that bore little resemblance to a real economy or a real city — and translating its genuine insights into real policy and intervention proved very weak. It also fragmented into many sub-fields that stopped talking to each other, and, most importantly for our purposes, it remained an external, God's-eye modelling stance: you stand outside and simulate the agents, which tells a manager or a citizen nothing about how to act within the system themselves. It explains, powerfully, why prediction and central planning fail in complex systems, but it hands you no practical method for acting in them.

Wicked problems — Rittel and Webber, 1973 (the diagnostic keystone; you were missing this)

The idea of wicked problems was set out by the design theorist Horst Rittel and his colleague Melvin Webber in "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning" in 1973, as a direct reaction to the repeated failure of rational planning and systems analysis on social problems. Its purpose was not to solve problems but to explain why a whole class of problems cannot be solved by technical planning at all, and thereby to account for why operations research and systems analysis kept failing on social issues. Its method is a characterisation rather than a procedure: it lists the properties that make a problem "wicked." There is no definitive formulation, because you cannot fully state the problem until you have already solved it. There is no stopping rule, because there is no natural point at which the problem is finished. There is no true-or-false test of a solution, only judgments of better or worse. Solutions are one-shot, because you cannot experiment on a society without leaving a permanent mark, so trial and error is ruled out. Every wicked problem is essentially unique. And every attempt to solve it changes the problem itself. The practical implication is that wicked problems call for argument and participation rather than calculation, which is why Rittel developed Issue-Based Information Systems, a way of mapping the issues, positions, and arguments in a debate. It "failed" as a milestone only in the sense that it is a diagnosis rather than a method, since it prescribes no procedure, and that is precisely why it feeds the participatory and design traditions rather than ending the search. Its lasting value is that it finally named the class of problem that all the earlier hard methods could not touch.

Actor-Network Theory — Latour, Callon, and Law, 1980s (you knew this)

Actor-network theory came out of science and technology studies, a field that studied scientists in their laboratories to understand how scientific facts are actually made, and it was developed by Latour, Callon, and Law and later synthesised in Latour's Science in Action in 1987 and Reassembling the Social in 2005. Its purpose was to explain how a scientific fact or a technological system comes to exist and, more importantly, how it holds together and comes to be accepted as true or working — by tracing the network of people, instruments, materials, and institutions that support it. The method is ethnographic: you "follow the actors" and describe what they actually do, and you insist on treating human and non-human elements symmetrically, so that a microscope, a sample, a grant, and a technician all count as participants that have to be enlisted. You then trace the process of "translation," in which one actor persuades others to join its network by redefining their interests so that serving the network serves their interests too; "inscription," in which intentions get built into physical objects so that the object enforces the behaviour (a speed bump inscribed with the intention that cars slow down); and "black-boxing," the point at which a whole stable network becomes treated as a single unquestioned object, so that a hard-won fact or a complex machine is simply taken for granted. It fell short because it is descriptive rather than prescriptive. It shows beautifully how networks form and hold together, but it offers no basis for judging whether a network is good or bad and no guidance for intervening to change one. Its insistence on treating people and objects alike, and its reluctance to take sides, make ethics and action difficult, and it is not operational for management. It is best read as a warning about how hard intervention really is, not as a tool for carrying it out.

Resilience, socio-ecological systems, and panarchy — Holling and Gunderson, 1973 to 2002 (you were missing this)

The resilience tradition began with the ecologist C. S. Holling's paper "Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems" in 1973, was carried forward by the Resilience Alliance, and was consolidated in Gunderson and Holling's book Panarchy in 2002. Its purpose was to understand how ecosystems, and the coupled systems of people and nature, manage to persist through shocks, adapt to change, and occasionally transform into something entirely new, and to manage them deliberately with that in mind. The method is built on two ideas. The "adaptive cycle" describes how any such system moves through four phases: a phase of rapid growth, a phase of conservation in which it becomes highly efficient but rigid and therefore fragile, a phase of release in which a disturbance collapses the accumulated structure and frees up resources, and a phase of reorganisation in which novelty can emerge before growth begins again. "Panarchy" then nests these cycles across scales, from a small patch to a whole region, and shows that a collapse at a small, fast scale can be contained, while a collapse at a large, slow scale can cascade down and take everything with it. On top of this, adaptive management treats every policy as an experiment: you act, you monitor carefully what happens, and you adjust as you learn. It fell short because it is diagnostic more than prescriptive, because the apparently neutral question of "resilience of what, and for whom" is deeply political, because the thresholds at which systems flip are very hard to measure in practice, and because it describes the dynamics without giving you a clear decision procedure for what to do next.

Cynefin — Dave Snowden, 1999 to 2007 (you were missing this)

Cynefin was developed by Dave Snowden during his work at IBM on complexity and knowledge management, and it reached a wide audience through "A Leader's Framework for Decision Making" in the Harvard Business Review in 2007. Its purpose was to give practitioners a simple way to work out what kind of situation they are facing and then to act in the way that situation demands, which effectively turns the whole history of the field into an everyday decision rule. The method divides situations into five domains, each with its own way of knowing and acting. In the "clear" domain, cause and effect are obvious to everyone, so you sense the situation, categorise it, and respond by applying the standard best practice. In the "complicated" domain, cause and effect are knowable but only through analysis and expertise, so you sense, analyse, and respond with expert advice. In the "complex" domain, cause and effect can only be seen in hindsight, so you cannot analyse your way to an answer; instead you run small, safe-to-fail experiments, probe, sense what happens, and respond by amplifying what works. In the "chaotic" domain, there is no discernible cause and effect at all, so you must act first to establish some stability, then sense and respond. The fifth domain, "confusion," is simply not yet knowing which of the others you are in, and the task there is to break the situation down until you can tell. The central rule is that you must not apply one domain's methods in another, and in particular you must not apply best practice or expert analysis to a genuinely complex problem. Cynefin falls short because it is under-specified, hard to validate empirically, and frequently misused as a rigid set of boxes, but its core insight — that the stance must match the situation, and that treating a complex problem as merely complicated is the root of failure — is the moral of the entire story.

Network science — Watts and Strogatz, 1998, and Barabási and Albert, 1999 (you were missing this)

Network science emerged from the meeting of graph theory, physics, and sociology, with Watts and Strogatz's work on small-world networks in 1998 and Barabási and Albert's work on scale-free networks in 1999, and it has since been applied from the internet to biology to social systems. Its purpose was to understand how the structure of the connections in a system shapes its behaviour — why some networks are robust, why some are fragile, and how things spread or cascade through them. The method treats a system as a graph of nodes and links and then measures its shape. The "small-world" finding is that adding just a few long-range links to a mostly local network collapses the distance between any two nodes to a handful of steps, which is the "six degrees of separation" effect. The "scale-free" finding is that many real networks have a few highly connected hubs and very many barely connected nodes, and this shape has a striking consequence: such networks are very robust to random failure, because removing a random node almost never hits a hub, but extremely fragile to a targeted attack on the hubs, which can shatter the network. Models of percolation and of epidemics then show how a contagion or a failure spreads or cascades through the structure. Network science fell short because it captures structure only and says nothing about meaning, agency, or purpose; because it is often very abstract; and because its predictive power for real, messy, coupled systems is limited. It is a powerful lens on how things are wired together, but not a method for deciding how to intervene.

Why each failure was inevitable

Every method in this history fails for the same underlying reason: each one freezes a single element that a genuinely complex system refuses to hold still. Operations research freezes the objective, assuming it is fixed and agreed. System dynamics freezes the model structure, assuming the boundaries and equations are the right ones. The viable system model freezes the purpose, assuming the organisation knows what it is for. Soft systems methodology freezes the set of stakeholders, assuming we know who they are and that they will negotiate in good faith. Actor-network theory freezes the network, describing it beautifully but unable to move it. Each of these is a lens, and every lens is also a blind spot, which is exactly what Ashby's law of requisite variety predicts: a method must possess at least as much variety as the problem it faces, and no single method ever does. This is why the endpoint of the story up to the 2000s is not one grand method but methodological pluralism, as expressed in critical systems thinking and in Cynefin — the admission that a wicked problem will always exceed any single tool, so you must combine several lenses and stay adaptive.

But the story does not stop in the 2000s — what happened in the 2010s and 2020s

Everything above explains the pattern of the single-method eras, and it ends around the 2000s. What has happened since roughly 2010 is different in character, and that difference is itself the most important thing to say. In the earlier eras each decade produced a dominant method that claimed to crack complexity and then failed into the next. Since about 2010 there has been no new grand method, and that is deliberate: the field has settled into a mature pluralism, combining tools, admitting the limits of prediction, and turning complexity thinking into practical decision-making under uncertainty. The advances of the period fall into a handful of themes.

The crisis of prediction, and the turn to "deep uncertainty"

The single most important intellectual shift of the period is the honest admission that for the most important complex systems we cannot predict, and the construction of decision tools that do not require prediction. The old dream was that a good enough model would tell you what will happen; the new realism, captured in Erica Thompson's 2022 book Escape from Model Land, is that every model is a simplified story and that we must be explicit about which stories we are telling and why. This gave rise to the idea of "model pluralism" — using several models built on different assumptions and looking at where they agree and disagree, rather than trusting one, which is exactly what happened publicly during the COVID-19 pandemic when rival epidemiological models were compared and their disagreements were themselves informative. Alongside this came a body of methods collected under the name decision-making under deep uncertainty. When you cannot assign probabilities to the future, you stop trying to find the single "optimal" policy and instead look for policies that are robust — that perform acceptably across many possible futures. Robust decision making, developed at RAND by Robert Lempert, generates thousands of plausible futures by computer and searches for options that avoid disaster in as many of them as possible; dynamic adaptive policy pathways, developed by Marjolijn Haasnoot and others, designs a sequence of decisions with built-in checkpoints, so you commit only to the next step and switch course when monitoring shows which future is actually unfolding. This is, in effect, the technical embodiment of the "probe, sense, respond" stance — the direct heir of the complexity turn, now made into concrete policy instruments used in climate adaptation and infrastructure planning.

The causal-inference revolution, and an honest reckoning about evidence

A second major development runs partly against the grain of complexity thinking and is therefore easy to miss. Over the same period, the "credibility revolution" in econometrics and statistics made enormous progress on the question of causation — how to establish that X actually causes Y rather than merely being correlated with it. Judea Pearl's work on causal diagrams and the do-calculus, popularised in The Book of Why in 2018, and the Nobel Prize in economics awarded to David Card, Joshua Angrist, and Guido Imbens in 2021 for their methods of causal inference, together changed what counts as rigorous evidence in the social sciences. This matters for complexity because it is the counterweight to the "we can never know anything" reading of complex systems: it says that even in a tangled world you can learn a great deal by looking at natural experiments, by randomising where you can, and by reasoning carefully about causal structure. It also produced its own backlash, and the backlash is itself a complexity insight. The randomised-controlled-trial movement promised to find "what works," but critics pointed out that a result which holds in one place and time often fails to generalise elsewhere, because the surrounding system differs — the problem of external validity. The lesson, now widely accepted, is that evidence is not a portable fact but a claim about a particular system, and that scaling a proven intervention to a new context is itself a complex problem rather than a mechanical copy-and-paste.

Network science matures into the science of systemic risk

Network science, which arrived in 1998 and 1999, grew up in this period and became the main quantitative language for talking about how complex systems fail. The study of cascading failures — how the failure of one node overloads its neighbours, which overload theirs, and so on until a whole system collapses — became central to understanding power grids, financial systems, and supply chains. The 2008 financial crisis was read through this lens as a contagion on a densely interconnected banking network, and it drove a lasting research programme on financial systemic risk. More recently the field has pushed into higher-order networks — models built not just from pairwise links but from groups, triangles, and larger structures (called simplicial complexes and hypergraphs) — because many real interactions involve three or more parties at once and cannot be captured by simple pairwise connections. This is directly relevant to the "system of systems" and polycrisis problems discussed earlier: it is the mathematics of how coupling between many systems produces fragility, cascades, and systemic risk.

Complexity meets data and artificial intelligence

The rise of big data and machine learning transformed how complexity is studied, in two directions at once. In one direction, machine learning became a powerful instrument for finding patterns in complex systems too complicated for hand-built models — from predicting protein structures to detecting anomalies in networks. In the other direction, the systems being studied became themselves algorithmic: recommender systems, social-media feeds, and search rankings are now genuine complex adaptive systems, shaping attention, opinion, and behaviour in ways that are emergent and only visible in aggregate, which launched the field of computational social science and the study of misinformation cascades and polarisation. More recently, digital twins — live computational replicas of real systems such as factories, cities, or infrastructure, continuously fed by sensor data — have become a practical engineering outgrowth, allowing operators to simulate and test interventions on a copy before touching the real thing. And since 2020 the arrival of large language models and other foundation models has opened a two-way street: these models are themselves complex systems, and researchers debate whether their capabilities "emerge" abruptly with scale or whether that appearance is an artefact of how we measure them, while at the same time the models are being used as tools to help model and reason about complex systems. This is the newest frontier and the most unsettled.

Complexity economics, and the turn to actually changing systems

Economists influenced by Santa Fe spent the period building an alternative to the equilibrium models that had dominated the field, using agent-based models in which an economy is simulated as many interacting firms and households rather than as a single set of equations assumed to be in balance, an approach whose credibility rose sharply after 2008 exposed the limits of mainstream macroeconomics. A striking applied success was economic complexity, developed by César Hidalgo and Ricardo Hausmann, which measures how many distinct, sophisticated products a country is able to make and uses the structure of that "product space" to predict future growth better than traditional indicators — a concrete demonstration that the shape of a complex system carries real predictive information. In parallel, and more oriented toward action, a whole field grew up around the question of how to deliberately transform complex systems rather than merely describe them. Socio-technical transitions theory, especially Frank Geels's "multi-level perspective," models how change actually happens: niche innovations struggle against an entrenched "regime" of technologies, institutions, and behaviours, until pressures from the broader "landscape" open a window and the niche breaks through — a framework now central to climate and energy policy. This fed into mission-oriented and transformative innovation policy, associated with Mariana Mazzucato and with Johan Schot and Ed Steinmueller, which argue that governments should set bold directional missions (a carbon-neutral economy, a cure for a disease) and shape markets toward them, rather than only fixing market failures. Kate Raworth's doughnut economics, which frames the goal as meeting everyone's basic needs without overshooting the planet's limits, and which was actually adopted by the city of Amsterdam in 2020, is the best-known public expression of this applied, values-laden turn.

Climate, the Earth system, and the science of tipping points

Complexity thinking became central to climate science through two ideas. The first is planetary boundaries, introduced by Johan Rockström and colleagues in 2009 and updated since, which identify the safe operating limits of the Earth system across climate, biodiversity, freshwater, and other dimensions; the 2023 "safe and just Earth system boundaries" work extended this to questions of justice. The second is climate tipping points, advanced by Tim Lenton and others, the recognition that parts of the climate system — ice sheets, rainforests, ocean circulation — can cross thresholds beyond which they flip abruptly and irreversibly, and that these tipping elements can cascade into one another. This is complexity science's most consequential real-world application, because it turns the abstract idea of nonlinearity and irreversibility into concrete, high-stakes policy questions, and it is precisely why the climate problem is the archetypal wicked problem.

Polycrisis, permacrisis, and the metacrisis — the 2020s framing

Finally, the 2020s produced a new vocabulary for the thing the earlier theory had only described in the abstract: multiple complex systems failing and interacting at once. The term polycrisis, revived and popularised by the economic historian Adam Tooze in 2022 (it goes back to Edgar Morin), names the situation in which several crises — pandemic, war, inflation, energy, food, climate — interact so that the combined effect is worse than the sum of the parts, because each worsens the others. The related words permacrisis and metacrisis capture the sense of a persistent, interlocking condition rather than a series of separate shocks. These are loose, journalistic terms rather than rigorous theory, and one should treat them with caution, but they are significant because they show the "system of systems" and "wicked problem" ideas of the 1970s finally entering mainstream public and policy language. The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020 to 2022 was, in effect, a live stress test that proved the point: it was a genuinely complex, coupled, deeply uncertain problem in which prediction failed, model pluralism was essential, policies had to be probed and adapted in real time, and the interactions between health, economy, and politics dominated the outcome.

The through-line of the whole period

What unites all of this is that the 2010s and 2020s did not produce another single method that then failed. Instead the field did three things at once. It grew humble about prediction and built real tools for acting under irreducible uncertainty. It married complexity thinking to data, computation, and artificial intelligence. And it turned from describing complex systems toward deliberately transforming them, through transitions theory, mission-oriented policy, and design. In that sense the period vindicates the endpoint described at the close of the earlier eras: the mature response to complexity is not a grand unified method but a disciplined pluralism — matching tools to situations, combining lenses, staying adaptive, and accepting that for wicked problems you can steer but never solve.

Q: from all the study mentioned above, what is the best approach to solve a complex problem?
A:
The honest answer is that the question contains the very trap the whole history diagnoses. Every era I described failed because it believed there was one best method, applied it to everything, and hit a wall the method could not climb. So the truthful answer is that there is no single best method, and the search for one is the mistake. But that is not a cop-out, because there is a best approach, and it is not a method at all — it is a disciplined way of choosing and combining methods and of acting when you cannot know in advance what will happen. If I had to compress everything above into one stance, it is this: diagnose the kind of problem before choosing a tool, frame it together with the people inside it, respect the power in it, combine several methods rather than worshipping one, act in small reversible steps and watch what happens, build the system's capacity to absorb surprise, work on leverage rather than control, learn continuously, and accept that at the top of the ladder you can steer but never solve. Let me unpack each of those moves, because each one is a direct lesson from a specific failure in the history.

First, diagnose the kind of problem before you touch a method. This is the lesson of Cynefin and of the cake, rocket, and child ladder. The most common and most expensive error in the entire history is treating a complex or wicked problem as if it were merely complicated — bringing a recipe to a child, or a plan to a society. So before you do anything, you ask: is this clear, where cause and effect are obvious and best practice will do? Is it complicated, where cause and effect are knowable through expert analysis? Or is it genuinely complex, where cause and effect will only be visible in hindsight? And beyond that, is it wicked, with contested goals and no stopping rule, like the seventeen goals? Getting this diagnosis wrong is what sank operations research on social problems and what makes leaders apply best practice to situations that have no best practice. The diagnosis costs almost nothing and determines everything downstream.

Second, frame the problem before you try to solve it, and frame it with the people who are in it. Operations research taught us that you cannot optimise a problem you have not correctly framed, and soft systems methodology taught us that framing is not a technical act — it is an act of interpretation, and different stakeholders will frame the same mess in incompatible ways. So the second move is to structure the problem rather than rush to solve it: to surface the competing worldviews, to make the different assumptions explicit, and to build a shared-enough picture of what the situation actually is. This is why the soft methods matter even when you ultimately use hard ones, because the hard methods are only as good as the framing that feeds them.

Third, respect the power and the conflict. The lesson of critical systems thinking is that not everyone in the room is equal, and that much of what looks like a neutral technical problem is actually a struggle between parties with unequal power. Soft methods that seek "accommodation" can quietly paper over domination, so the honest approach names the power, asks who benefits and who bears the cost, and does not pretend that consensus is always possible or always desirable. Ignoring this is how well-meaning participatory exercises end up ratifying the interests of whoever was already strongest.

Fourth, combine methods rather than worship one. This is Ashby's law of requisite variety applied to practice: your approach must have at least as much variety as the problem, and no single method ever does. So the mature practitioner assembles a toolkit and matches the tool to the part of the problem it suits — hard optimisation for the genuinely tractable sub-problems, soft structuring for the contested parts, critical analysis for the coercive parts, and complexity tools for the emergent parts. This is exactly what methodological pluralism and Cynefin's "match your stance to the domain" were pointing at. The enemy here is not any particular method but method-worship — the belief that your favourite lens is the whole truth.

Fifth, act in small reversible steps and watch what happens. Because you cannot predict a complex system, you cannot plan the whole journey in advance, so the fifth move is to probe, sense, and respond. You run small, safe-to-fail experiments rather than one big irreversible bet; you monitor carefully what actually happens; and you amplify what works and dampen what does not. When the uncertainty is deep enough that you cannot even assign probabilities, you reach for the tools that do not require prediction — robust decision making that looks for options that survive across many possible futures, and dynamic adaptive policy pathways that commit only to the next step and switch course as the future reveals itself. This is the technical form of humility: you act, but you keep the ability to change your mind cheaply.

Sixth, build the system's capacity to absorb surprise rather than optimising it into fragility. The lesson of resilience and of the adaptive cycle is that systems which become maximally efficient and tightly coupled also become brittle, because they have removed all the slack and diversity that would let them absorb a shock. So a good approach deliberately preserves redundancy, diversity, modularity, and slack, and it watches for the moment when a system has grown so rigid in its "conservation" phase that a small disturbance could trigger a collapse. The goal is not a perfectly tuned machine, because perfectly tuned machines shatter; it is a system that can bend, absorb, and reorganise.

Seventh, work on leverage rather than trying to control the whole. The lesson of transitions theory and of network science is that you cannot steer a complex system by pulling one big lever, because there is no single lever and the couplings will surprise you. Instead you look for the places where a small, well-placed intervention propagates — the hubs in a network, the niches where a new approach can grow until the surrounding regime is ready to shift, the leverage points where the system's own dynamics will carry your push further than your effort alone. Mission-oriented policy and socio-technical transitions thinking are the mature forms of this move: you set a direction, you nurture niches, and you shape the landscape rather than micromanaging the whole.

Eighth, learn continuously, but remember that evidence is not portable. The lesson of adaptive management and of the causal-inference revolution together is that you should treat every policy as an experiment, measure what actually happens, and adjust — but also that a result which worked in one place and time may fail elsewhere, because the surrounding system differs. So you combine rigorous causal learning with a humility about external validity, and you treat scaling a proven intervention as itself a complex problem rather than a copy-and-paste.

Ninth, accept that you can only steer, never solve. The lesson of wicked problems is that for the hardest problems there is no stopping rule, no true-or-false test, and no point at which the problem is finished, because every attempt to fix it changes it. So the honest endpoint is provisional, revisable, good-enough action, taken by agreement, with the expectation that you will keep adjusting. The fantasy of the clean solution is itself a symptom of having misdiagnosed the problem as complicated when it was complex or wicked.

Pulling it together, the best approach to a complex problem is therefore not a method but a disciplined loop, and it can be stated in a single sentence: sense what kind of problem you are facing, frame it with the people inside it, respect the power in it, combine the right tools, act in small reversible steps, watch what happens and adapt, build the system's capacity to absorb surprise, work on leverage rather than control, learn continuously, and accept that you can steer but never solve. Everything the field learned across seventy years points at this same conclusion from different directions, and the deepest lesson of all is the one that closes the loop: the moment you believe you have found the one best method that will finally crack complexity is precisely the moment you have stopped doing it.
